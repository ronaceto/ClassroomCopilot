This part introduces the foundation students need before using AI more actively: what

===== PAGE 7 =====
Chapter 1 — Understanding Artificial Intelligence 
 
Key Question 
What is artificial intelligence, and why does it matter for students today? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand what artificial intelligence is in simple terms 
 Recognize how AI is already part of everyday life 
 Explore how AI can support learning in school 
 Begin thinking about AI as a tool that should strengthen, not replace, human 
thinking 
Introduction 
Artificial intelligence is no longer something that belongs only in science fiction movies 
or advanced technology labs. It is already part of everyday life. 
Students use systems powered by AI when they search online, stream music, watch 
recommended videos, use navigation apps, or interact with voice assistants. In many 
cases, people use artificial intelligence every day without even realizing it. These tools 
work quietly in the background, helping to sort information, suggest content, and make 
digital systems feel faster and more personal. 
What makes this moment different is that students can now interact directly with AI in 
ways that previous generations could not. Instead of only receiving recommendations or 
automated suggestions, students can now ask questions, request explanations, generate 
ideas, and explore topics through conversation with AI tools. 
That creates both an opportunity and a challenge. The opportunity is clear: AI can help 
students learn faster, ask better questions, and explore ideas in new ways. The challenge 
is just as important: students must learn how to use these tools wisely. If AI is used only 
to get answers quickly, it can weaken learning. If it is used as a tool for thinking, it can 
strengthen curiosity, creativity, and understanding.

===== PAGE 8 =====
What Is Artificial Intelligence? 
Artificial intelligence, often shortened to AI, refers to computer systems designed to 
perform tasks that usually require human intelligence. These tasks include understanding 
language, identifying patterns, generating responses, solving problems, and making 
predictions. 
That definition may sound technical, but the basic idea is simple. AI systems are built to 
process information in ways that imitate certain parts of human thinking. They do not 
think like people, and they do not understand the world in the same way humans do, but 
they can still perform useful tasks that make them seem surprisingly smart. 
For students, the most important thing to understand is that AI is not magic. It is a tool 
created by people, built using data and algorithms, and designed to respond to questions 
or tasks in ways that seem helpful and intelligent. 
AI Is Already Part of Students’ Lives 
One reason AI matters so much is that it is already woven into the tools students use 
every day. When a streaming platform recommends a movie, AI may be involved. When 
a phone suggests the next word in a text message, AI may be involved. When a search 
engine predicts what someone is trying to find, AI may be involved. Even photo apps, 
translation tools, and spam filters often rely on AI systems in some form. 
Because these systems are so common, students are growing up in a world where AI is 
becoming a normal part of learning, communication, and decision-making. That means 
AI literacy is becoming just as important as digital literacy. 
Why AI Matters in Education 
In school, AI can be a powerful academic support tool. A student who is confused 
about a science concept can ask for a simpler explanation. A student preparing for a 
history quiz can ask for practice questions. A student working on an essay can 
brainstorm ideas, test an argument, or ask for feedback on clarity and organization. 
This does not mean AI should replace teachers, textbooks, or classroom instruction. 
Teachers provide guidance, judgment, structure, and human understanding that AI 
cannot replace. Textbooks provide curated knowledge and reliable organization. AI 
works best when it is used alongside these resources, not instead of them.

===== PAGE 9 =====
AI as a Tool for Thinking 
The most important idea in this chapter is that AI should be understood as a tool for 
thinking, not a shortcut for avoiding thought. There is a major difference between a 
student who uses AI to deepen understanding and a student who uses AI only to 
complete work faster. 
For example, one student might ask AI to explain the water cycle step by step, provide 
an analogy, and then generate a few quiz questions. Another student might simply ask 
AI to answer a homework question and then copy the response. Both students used AI, 
but only one of them used it in a way that supports learning. 
Student Example 
Imagine a student named Maya who is studying biology and struggling to understand 
photosynthesis. She asks for an AI tool to explain photosynthesis in simple terms for a 
high school student, then follows up with a request for an everyday analogy and a short 
practice quiz. Within a few minutes, she has an explanation, a memory aid, and a mini 
review activity. What made the difference was not just the tool. It was the way she used 
it. 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can ask students to list places where artificial intelligence already appears in 
their daily lives, streaming recommendations, navigation apps, search tools, social media 
feeds, text prediction, and translation tools. From there, the class can discuss which of 
these tools help people learn, which influence decisions, and why understanding AI 
matters beyond the classroom.

Chapter 2 — How AI Actually Works 
 
Key Question 
How do artificial intelligence systems generate responses, and why do they sometimes 
make mistakes? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand the basic idea behind how AI systems are trained 
 Learn how AI uses patterns and prediction to generate responses 
 Recognize why AI can sound confident even when it is wrong 
 Begin thinking more critically about what AI can and cannot do 
Introduction 
For many students, artificial intelligence can feel mysterious. You type a question into a 
chat window, and within seconds the system gives you an answer that sounds organized, 
detailed, and surprisingly human. It may explain a science concept, summarize a 
historical event, or help brainstorm an essay topic. Because the response sounds fluent 
and confident, it is easy to assume the AI truly understands what it is saying. 
To use AI well, students need to understand an important truth: AI does not think the 
way humans do. It does not “know” things in the way a teacher, student, or scientist 
knows them. Instead, it works by learning patterns from massive amounts of 
information and then using those patterns to predict what kind of response is most 
likely to make sense. 
This chapter explains that process in simple terms. You do not need to know advanced 
computer science to understand the big idea. What matters is knowing enough to use AI 
thoughtfully, especially when the information it gives you sounds convincing.

===== PAGE 13 =====
Training AI Systems 
Artificial intelligence systems do not begin with human-like understanding. They are 
built and trained using enormous amounts of data. 
This training data may include books, articles, websites, research papers, conversations, 
and many other forms of written language. During training, the AI system examines 
these materials and looks for relationships between words, phrases, and ideas. Over 
time, it becomes very good at recognizing patterns in how language is used. 
For example, if the system sees many examples of how teachers explain gravity, or how 
textbooks describe the causes of World War I, it begins to recognize how those ideas are 
commonly expressed. It does not memorize information in the same way a student 
studies for a test. Instead, it becomes skilled at recognizing what kinds of words and 
phrases tend to go together. 
This is one reason AI can seem so impressive. It has analyzed far more language than 
any one person ever could. But that does not mean it has wisdom, judgment, or true 
understanding. 
Pattern Recognition 
At the heart of modern AI is pattern recognition. 
Pattern recognition means identifying repeated relationships within information. Human 
beings do this too. For example, after solving enough algebra problems, a student begins 
to recognize patterns in how equations work. After reading enough essays, a writer 
begins to notice patterns in strong introductions and conclusions. 
AI works in a similar way, but on a much larger scale. 
Instead of learning from a few examples, AI systems learn from enormous numbers of 
examples. They identify how people phrase questions, how ideas are usually connected, 
and how explanations are typically structured. When you ask the AI a question, it uses 
those patterns to generate a response that seems relevant and coherent. 
This is why AI can often explain topics clearly, write in different tones, or generate 
content that sounds polished. It has learned what those patterns usually look like.

===== PAGE 14 =====
Prediction, Not Understanding 
AI generates responses by prediction, not by understanding. 
When you type a prompt into an AI system, it does not pause and think like a person 
would. It does not reflect on its own experiences or reason through the meaning in the 
same way a human mind does. Instead, it predicts what words are most likely to come 
next based on the patterns it has learned. 
By predicting one word at a time, and then the next, and then the next, AI can build 
long responses that sound fluent and useful. This process happens very quickly, which is 
why AI tools can respond in seconds. 
A good way to think about it is this: AI is extremely good at generating language that 
sounds right, but that is different from actually knowing whether the content is true.  
That difference matters. 
Why AI Sometimes Makes Mistakes 
Because AI works by prediction, it can sometimes produce responses that sound correct 
but are actually wrong. 
An AI hallucination happens when the system generates false, inaccurate, or invented 
information in a confident tone. For example, an AI tool might: 
 give an incorrect date for a historical event 
 invent a quotation that never existed 
 describe a scientific process inaccurately 
 create a fake source or reference 
This can be dangerous if students assume the response must be correct simply because it 
sounds polished. 
The problem is not that AI is useless. The problem is that AI is not designed to care 
about truth in the way humans do. Its goal is to generate a response that fits the 
language patterns of the prompt. Sometimes that response is excellent. Sometimes it is 
flawed. 
That is why students must always bring their own judgment to the process.

===== PAGE 15 =====
AI Is Not a Search Engine 
Many students compare AI tools to search engines, but the two are not the same.  A 
search engine usually finds existing information and links you to sources. An AI tool 
generates a brand-new response based on patterns in data. That makes AI feel more 
conversational, but it also means you must be more careful.  When you use a search 
engine, you can inspect the websites it found. When you use AI, you are often receiving 
a polished answer without immediately seeing where the information came from. 
AI is often best used for: 
 explanations 
 summaries 
 brainstorming 
 practice questions 
 idea generation 
Search engines and reliable sources are still essential for checking facts, verifying claims, 
and doing formal research. 
A Student Example 
A student named Jordan asks AI to explain the main causes of World War I. The 
response sounds clear and organized, but instead of trusting it immediately, Jordan 
checks it against class notes and a textbook. The explanation is mostly correct, but one 
part oversimplifies the role of alliances and leaves out an important detail discussed in 
class. By checking the response, Jordan gets the benefit of a quick explanation while still 
relying on trusted sources for accuracy. This is exactly how students should think about 
AI: it can be a useful starting point, but it is not the final authority. 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can help students understand how AI works by comparing AI-generated 
responses to textbook explanations. 
For example, students could ask AI to explain a topic such as photosynthesis, the Civil 
War, or linear equations. Then they could compare that explanation with what appears 
in their class materials. 
This activity helps students see that AI can be useful, but it also helps them practice 
identifying missing details, oversimplifications, and possible inaccuracies.

Chapter 3 — AI as a Learning Partner 
 
Key Question 
How can students use artificial intelligence to support learning instead of simply getting 
answers? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand the difference between using AI for answers and using AI for 
learning 
 Recognize how AI can support curiosity, practice, and understanding 
 Learn how follow-up questions improve learning 
 Begin thinking of AI as a study partner rather than a shortcut 
Introduction 
When students first begin using artificial intelligence, many treat it like a faster search 
engine or a homework shortcut. They type in a question, read the answer, and move on. 
That may feel efficient, but it often leads to shallow learning. The real value of AI is not 
that it can give answers quickly. The real value is that it can help students think more 
deeply, ask better questions, and explore ideas in ways that are more interactive than a 
textbook or worksheet alone. 
A good learning partner does not simply hand over answers. A good learning partner 
helps clarify ideas, supports practice, asks useful questions, and makes it easier to 
understand where confusion begins. AI can play that role well—if students use it 
intentionally.

===== PAGE 19 =====
The Difference Between Answers and Understanding 
There is a big difference between receiving an answer and actually understanding 
something. Imagine two students preparing for a science quiz. The first student asks AI 
for the definition of photosynthesis, reads the result, and moves on. The second asks for 
the concept to be explained in simple terms, followed by an example and a quiz 
question. Both students used AI, but only one used it in a way that supports deeper 
learning. That distinction matters. A student who only collects answers may finish faster, 
but a student who uses AI to explore and practice is much more likely to remember and 
understand the material later. 
AI as a Thinking Partner 
One of the most useful ways to use AI is as a tool for extending thought. Students can 
ask AI to explain difficult ideas in simpler language, compare two concepts, create 
examples, generate practice questions, offer alternate explanations, or help organize 
understanding before a test. For example, if a student is struggling with the causes of the 
French Revolution, they might begin by asking for the main causes in simple terms, then 
follow up by asking which cause mattered most and why, and finally ask for a few quiz 
questions based on the explanation. This kind of interaction turns AI into something 
closer to a tutor or study coach. 
Why Follow-Up Questions Matter 
One of the biggest advantages of AI is that it allows students to continue the learning 
process through follow-up questions. A textbook explanation is fixed, and a teacher may 
not always be available at the exact moment a student gets confused. AI, by contrast, lets 
students keep asking until the explanation becomes more useful and more clear. A 
student can ask for a simpler explanation, request an example, compare the idea to 
something familiar, ask to be quizzed, or ask what students often misunderstand about 
the topic. That flexibility is one reason AI has become so powerful in education: 
students are not forced into one style of explanation and can keep refining the support 
until it helps them learn best. 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can use this chapter to help students see how AI becomes most useful when it 
supports practice rather than just explanation. For example, after asking for a simple 
explanation of the water cycle, students could ask AI to create five quiz questions and 
then explain any missed answers. That turns AI from an answer source into a learning 
system built around explanation, practice, feedback, and improvement. 
Learning does not happen just because a student reads a clear explanation. Learning 
becomes stronger when students practice what they are trying to understand.

Chapter 4 — The Power of Good Prompts 
 
Key Question 
How does the way you ask a question affect the quality of an AI response? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand what a prompt is 
 Learn why clear prompts produce better results 
 Recognize the parts of an effective prompt 
 Practice improving weak prompts 
Introduction 
Artificial intelligence tools can be incredibly helpful, but they are only as useful as the 
instructions they receive. If you ask a vague question, you will usually get a vague 
answer. If you ask a thoughtful, specific question, the response is much more likely to be 
useful. Prompting is not just a technical skill. It is also a thinking skill, because writing a 
good prompt requires students to know what they want, what they need help with, and 
how to guide the tool in the right direction. 
What Is a Prompt? 
A prompt is the instruction, question, or request you give to an AI tool. Some prompts 
are short and simple, but stronger prompts give the AI more direction by defining the 
topic, audience, and format of the response. 
Compare these two prompts: “Explain climate change.” and “Explain climate change in 
simple terms for a high school student and include three examples of real-world effects.” 
The second version is better because it gives the AI a clearer job to do. 
Why Better Prompts Matter 
Good prompts help students get more accurate explanations, save time by reducing 
confusion, receive answers at the right difficulty level, and generate more useful 
examples. In school, that means stronger studying, clearer writing, and more targeted 
feedback.

===== PAGE 25 =====
The Prompt Pyramid 
A helpful way to think about prompting is to imagine a pyramid. At the bottom are very 
simple prompts. As you move upward, prompts become more powerful because they 
include more structure and more useful detail. 
Basic prompt: “Explain the water cycle.” Better prompt: “Explain the water cycle step 
by step.” Strong prompt: “Explain the water cycle step by step for a middle school 
student and include a simple real-world example.” 
 
Figure 4.1 — The Prompt Pyramid 
This diagram shows how prompts become stronger as students add context, task details, 
audience, and formatting instructions. 
 
The Four Parts of a Strong Prompt 
Strong prompts often include four parts: context, task, audience, and format. Context 
tells the AI what situation matters. The task explains what you want it to do. The 
audience tells the AI who the explanation is for. The format tells the AI how to organize 
the answer.

===== PAGE 26 =====
How Students Can Improve Their Prompts 
You do not need to write the perfect prompt on your first try. Strong AI users often 
improve prompts as they go. A student might begin with a basic question, realize the 
answer is too broad, and ask again with more detail. That is not failure. It is refinement. 
Student Example 
A student preparing for a quiz on the American Revolution first asks, “Tell me about 
the American Revolution.” The answer is broad and not especially helpful. They revise it 
to: “Explain the three main causes of the American Revolution in simple terms for a 
high school student and end with three quiz questions.” The response becomes more 
focused and directly useful for studying. 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can give students the same weak prompt and ask them to improve it in pairs or 
small groups. This helps students see that prompt writing is really about precision, 
purpose, and communication. 
Try This 
Use the two prompts below to compare how AI changes its explanation when you ask 
for a different kind of response. Then think carefully about what still needs verification 
and which version actually helped you understand the concept more clearly. 
Prompt Example 
Create a study guide for the causes of World War I suitable for a high school student. 
 
Prompt Example

Chapter Summary 
In this chapter, you learned that prompting is one of the most important AI skills 
students can develop. You explored what prompts are, why stronger prompts lead to 
better results, and how context, task, audience, and format make AI responses more 
useful.

===== PAGE 27 =====
Chapter 5 — AI for Research and Critical 
Thinking 
 
Key Question 
How can students use AI to support research while still thinking critically and verifying 
information? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI can help students begin and organize research 
 Learn how AI can support question generation and topic exploration 
 Recognize why verification is essential when using AI for research 
 Use AI to strengthen critical thinking rather than replace it 
Introduction 
Whether students are writing an essay, preparing a presentation, working on a history 
project, or exploring a science topic, research helps them gather information, examine 
evidence, and build understanding. Strong research is not just about finding facts. It is 
about asking good questions, organizing information clearly, and deciding what is 
trustworthy. 
AI can help students get a quick overview of a topic, generate useful questions, organize 
notes, compare perspectives, and identify areas that need deeper investigation. But AI 
should not replace careful thinking. In fact, research is one of the clearest examples of 
why students need to use AI thoughtfully. 
A polished AI response may sound complete, but that does not mean it is fully accurate, 
well-sourced, or detailed enough for schoolwork. This is why students should think of 
AI as a research assistant, not as the final authority.

===== PAGE 28 =====
Using AI to Start Research 
One of the best uses of AI in research is at the beginning of the process, when students 
need help understanding the big picture. For example, a student beginning a project on 
the Industrial Revolution might ask for an overview of the topic and why it changed 
society. A prompt like that can help the student identify major themes before moving 
into more detailed sources. AI is especially useful for generating background knowledge, 
surfacing key terms, and helping students see possible angles for a project or essay. 
Generating Better Research Questions 
Strong research depends on strong questions. 
Many students begin with topics that are too broad. For example, “World War II” is not 
really a research question. It is a huge topic. A better research process begins when 
students narrow their focus and ask something more specific. 
AI can help with that. 
A student might ask: 
Help me narrow my topic on climate change into a question suitable for a school 
presentation. 
This is one of AI’s most useful roles in research. It can help students move from a broad 
topic toward a clearer, more manageable question. That shift improves the quality of the 
entire project. 
Exploring Perspectives 
Good research often involves looking at more than one point of view. 
This is especially important in subjects such as history, government, literature, and social

The AI Time Machine 
One especially engaging research strategy is to use AI to simulate historical or

This approach allows students to ask questions as though they were speaking directly to

This type of simulated interview helps students imagine historical events as lived

better questioning.

Why Verification Matters 
This is where AI research must be handled carefully. 
AI can generate a smooth and convincing answer even when part of the information is 
inaccurate, incomplete, or oversimplified. A student who relies entirely on AI without 
checking other sources may end up repeating errors without realizing it. 
That is why verification is essential.

 trusted educational websites

 Is this explanation accurate? 
 What may be missing here? 
 Does this match what my class materials say?

Introduction 
Many students think studying means reading the same notes again and again until the

The problem is not always effort. Sometimes the problem is the method.

When used correctly, AI can act like a study coach. It can create practice questions,

Key Question 
How can students use AI to support writing, revision, and communication without

Learning Objectives

 Learn how AI can help organize ideas, strengthen clarity, and adjust writing for

 Recognize the difference between using AI for feedback and using AI to replace

 Protect student voice while improving communication across school

Introduction 
Writing can be one of the most challenging parts of school. 
Students are often asked to turn ideas into essays, reports, stories, reflections, and 
presentations. Even when they understand the topic, many still struggle with how to 
begin. They may stare at a blank page, unsure of how to organize their thoughts, how to 
build a strong argument, or how to make their writing more clear and engaging. 
This is one area where artificial intelligence can be especially helpful when students are 
shaping ideas into clear, effective writing. 
AI can support writing by helping students brainstorm starting points, organize 
information, revise for clarity, and improve how ideas are communicated to a reader or 
audience. 
However, writing is also an area where students must be especially careful. If AI begins 
doing too much of the thinking, planning, or drafting, then students lose something 
important: the chance to develop their own voice. 
This chapter is about using AI to support writing, revision, and communication in ways 
that strengthen student thinking rather than replace it.

===== PAGE 41 =====
AI as a Brainstorming Partner 
One of the hardest parts of writing is simply getting started. 
Students may know the general topic of an assignment but still feel uncertain about what 
angle to take, what claim to make, or what ideas are worth developing. In those 
moments, AI can act like a brainstorming partner. 
For example, a student writing about the impact of social media might ask: 
Help me brainstorm three possible arguments about how social media affects teenagers. 
That kind of prompt does not ask AI to write the essay. It asks AI to generate starting 
points. 
This is an important distinction. 
Used well, AI can help students: 
 generate possible essay angles 
 identify pros and cons of an issue 
 suggest examples that support a topic 
 turn a broad idea into a narrower focus 
 overcome the fear of a blank page 
The goal of brainstorming is not to let AI decide what the student thinks. It is to 
generate possibilities so the student can choose, shape, and develop ideas more 
confidently. In that sense, AI works best as a starting-point generator rather than as a 
substitute for real writing decisions. 
Turning Ideas Into Outlines 
Once students have a direction, the next challenge is organization. 
A good piece of writing needs structure. It should have a clear beginning, logical 
development, and a strong ending. Many students understand their topic but struggle 
with how to arrange their ideas into a coherent outline. 
AI can help with this stage too. 
A student might ask: 
Create an outline for a persuasive essay about renewable energy for a high school 
student.

===== PAGE 42 =====
The response may provide a basic structure: 
 introduction 
 main claim 
 supporting points 
 counterargument 
 conclusion 
That kind of outline can be helpful because it gives students a framework to build on. 
But the student still needs to evaluate and adapt it. The outline should be a guide, not a 
script. 
Strong writing still depends on student judgment. Students must decide which ideas 
matter most, which examples fit best, and how they want to present their own thinking. 
Strengthening Writing Without Replacing It 
AI can also support revision. 
This may be one of its most useful roles in writing. Students often have a draft, but they 
are not sure how to make it stronger. They may know the writing feels weak in some 
way, but they cannot yet identify whether the problem is clarity, organization, repetition, 
or weak evidence. 
In those situations, AI can act like a feedback partner. 
For example, a student might ask: 
Review this paragraph and suggest ways to improve clarity. 
Or: 
Tell me how I can make this argument stronger without changing my main idea. 
These are strong uses of AI because they keep the student’s writing at the center. The 
AI is not replacing the writer. It is helping the writer revise more effectively. 
This approach is very different from asking: 
Write my essay about renewable energy. 
The second prompt turns AI into a substitute. The first prompts turn AI into a tool for 
improvement. 
That difference matters.

Protecting Student Voice 
Student writing should still sound like the student. 
This is one of the most important rules in this chapter. 
A strong essay is not just correct. It reflects the student’s thinking, word choice, and 
interpretation. If AI rewrites too much, the work may begin to sound polished but 
generic. It may lose the personality, originality, or perspective that makes student writing 
meaningful. 
That is why students should use AI carefully when revising. It is fine to ask for 
suggestions, examples, or alternate ways to phrase something, but the final writing 
should still feel like the student’s own work. 
A useful way to think about it is this: 
AI can help you become a stronger writer. 
AI should not become your replacement writer.

These kinds of prompts help students think more carefully about audience, structure, 
and communication. AI can be especially helpful when students are trying to make their 
writing clearer or more effective.

Chapter 8 — AI for Problem Solving 
 
Key Question 
How can students use AI to solve problems more effectively without depending on it 
for every answer? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI can support step-by-step problem solving 
 Learn how AI can help students recognize patterns in math and science 
 Use AI to clarify confusing steps and strengthen reasoning 
 Practice using AI as a guide rather than a shortcut 
Introduction 
Many students find problem solving frustrating. 
This is especially true in subjects like math, physics, chemistry, and economics, where 
one wrong step can make an entire answer fall apart. Even when students understand 
the topic in general, they may still struggle to figure out what to do first, how to move 
from one step to the next, or why a solution works the way it does. 
This is one area where AI can be genuinely helpful. 
Artificial intelligence can break down complicated problems, explain processes step by 
step, and offer examples that help students see patterns more clearly. It can also respond 
to follow-up questions in a way that feels more flexible than a worksheet or textbook. 
But there is a risk here too. 
If students use AI only to get the final answer, they may finish faster, but they often 
learn less. The real value of AI in problem solving is not the answer itself. It is the 
support it gives students while they learn the process. 
This chapter is about using AI to strengthen reasoning, not replace it.

===== PAGE 48 =====
Why Problem Solving Feels Hard 
Many school problems are difficult not because the topic is impossible, but because the 
path is unclear. 
A student may know the formula for slope, understand the definition of velocity, or 
remember the parts of an equation, but still feel stuck when facing an unfamiliar 
problem. What students often need most is not just a correct answer. They need help 
seeing the structure of the problem. 
This is where AI can help. 
A student might ask: 
Show me how to solve this algebra problem step by step and explain why each step 
works. 
That kind of prompt does something important. It shifts the focus away from “What is 
the answer?” and toward “How does this work?” 
That shift is where real learning happens. 
Learning the Process, Not Just the Result 
Strong problem solving depends on understanding the process. 
If a student only copies the answer, the learning is shallow and temporary. But if the 
student sees how the problem is set up, how the numbers relate to one another, and why 
each step leads to the next, then the student is much more likely to solve a similar 
problem independently later. 
AI can support that by explaining: 
 which information matters 
 what step should come first 
 why a formula applies 
 what common mistakes to avoid 
 how to check whether the answer makes sense 
For example, a student working on a physics problem might ask: 
Explain how to solve this velocity problem step by step and tell me why each formula is 
used. 
A student working on geometry might ask: 
Help me understand why this triangle problem uses the Pythagorean theorem.

===== PAGE 49 =====
These are strong uses of AI because they keep the student focused on understanding the 
reasoning. 
Using AI to Recognize Patterns 
One of the most important parts of problem solving is pattern recognition. 
Students become stronger problem solvers when they begin to notice that many 
problems follow similar structures. In algebra, for example, certain types of equations 
are solved in familiar ways. In chemistry, balancing equations involves repeated logic. In 
economics, supply-and-demand scenarios often rely on recurring relationships. 
AI can help students recognize these patterns by showing multiple examples and 
comparing them. 
For example, a student could ask: 
Show me three examples of how to solve systems of equations and explain what pattern 
stays the same each time. 
Or: 
Compare these two chemistry problems and explain what strategy they have in common. 
This kind of use is powerful because students are not just solving one problem. They are 
learning how to see the structure behind many problems. 
That is what helps them improve over time.

A stronger prompt might be:

An even better prompt might be:

similar problem to try on my own.

Avoiding Shortcut Learning 
This chapter may be one of the easiest places for students to misuse AI. 
A student facing a difficult problem may be tempted to paste it into an AI tool, copy the 
answer, and move on. That can feel efficient in the short term, but it weakens learning 
over time. 
The problem is not just academic honesty, though that matters. The deeper issue is that 
copying solutions prevents students from developing confidence and independence. 
Students do not become better problem solvers by reading correct answers. They 
become better problem solvers by practicing, making mistakes, asking questions, and 
gradually building skill. 
AI should make that process easier to access, not easier to avoid. 
A good rule is this: 
If AI gives you the answer, ask it to teach you the process. If it teaches you the 
process, ask for a new problem so you can try it yourself. 
That sequence turns AI from a shortcut into a learning tool.

Chapter 9 — AI for Coding Help 
 
Key Question 
How can students use AI to learn coding more effectively without becoming dependent 
on it? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI can support students who are learning to code 
 Learn how AI can help explain code, debug errors, and suggest next steps 
 Recognize the difference between learning from AI and copying from AI

Introduction 
Learning to code can be exciting, but it can also be frustrating. 
Students often start programming with curiosity and energy, only to run into error 
messages, confusing logic problems, or code that almost works but not quite. A missing 
bracket, a misspelled variable name, or one incorrect line can stop an entire program 
from running. For beginners, this can make coding feel overwhelming very quickly. 
This is one of the reasons AI can be so useful in computer science. 
AI tools can help students understand what code is doing, explain why an error is 
happening, suggest possible fixes, and guide them through the process of building small 
programs. When used well, AI can act like a coding coach that helps students keep 
moving instead of getting stuck for long periods of time. 
But this is also an area where students need to be thoughtful. 
If a student pastes in a programming task and simply copies the finished solution, very 
little learning takes place. The code may work, but the student has not developed the 
logic, vocabulary, or debugging habits that programming is supposed to teach. 
This chapter is about using AI to support coding in a way that builds understanding and 
confidence.

===== PAGE 55 =====
Why Coding Feels Different From Other Subjects 
Coding is different from many other school tasks because students are not just learning 
facts. They are learning a system. 
To write code successfully, students need to understand: 
 how instructions are structured 
 how a program flows step by step 
 how variables, loops, and functions behave 
 how to test and revise a solution when something goes wrong 
This makes coding highly practical. Students often learn best by trying, failing, adjusting, 
and trying again. 
That process can be very productive, but it can also be discouraging when students do 
not understand why something failed. AI can help by making those moments of 
confusion easier to work through. 
Using AI to Understand Code 
One of the most helpful uses of AI in coding is asking it to explain code clearly. 
A student might paste in a short program and ask: 
Explain what this code does step by step. 
That kind of prompt is valuable because it turns code into something more 
understandable. Instead of staring at syntax that feels confusing, the student receives a 
guided explanation of what each part is doing and how the whole program works. 
Students can also ask more specific questions such as: 
 What does this variable do? 
 Why is this loop important? 
 What happens if I change this line? 
 Can you explain this like I’m a beginner? 
These questions help students move from copying code to understanding code. 
That shift is one of the most important goals in computer science education.

===== PAGE 56 =====
Using AI to Debug Errors 
Another major use of AI in coding is debugging. 
Debugging means identifying and fixing problems in code. This is a normal part of 
programming. In fact, learning to debug is one of the most important programming 
skills students can develop. 
AI can help students debug by explaining: 
 what an error message means 
 which line may be causing the problem 
 why the logic is not working 
 what kinds of fixes to try next 
For example, a student might ask: 
I’m getting an error in this Python code. Explain what the error means and what I 
should check. 
This kind of prompt is much stronger than asking for a finished answer. It keeps the 
student involved in the debugging process and encourages them to understand the cause 
of the problem. 
Students can also ask: 
 Why does this code not work as expected? 
 What is one possible bug in this function? 
 Can you point me to the part I should recheck without rewriting everything? 
These are the kinds of questions that build real coding skill. 
AI as a Coding Coach 
The strongest way to use AI in programming is to treat it as a coach. 
A coach does not play the game for you. A coach helps you improve your skills, spot 
mistakes, and understand what to do next. AI can play that role well in coding because 
programming often requires immediate feedback and repeated practice. 
For example, a student building a simple calculator app might ask: 
Help me build a simple calculator in Python and explain each step as we go. 
Then, after trying a section alone, the student could ask: 
Check this function and tell me whether the logic makes sense.

It also reinforces an important habit: coding is a process of building, testing, revising,

The Risk of Overdependence 
Because AI can generate working code quickly, students may be tempted to let it do too 
much of the work. 
This is where coding with AI can become risky. 
A student who copies large sections of AI-generated code without understanding what 
the code does may complete an assignment, but they will not build the habits that real 
programmers need. When the next assignment comes, or when the code breaks in a 
slightly different way, that student may not know how to proceed. 
This is why students should regularly ask themselves: 
 Do I understand what this code does? 
 Could I explain it to someone else? 
 Could I modify it on my own? 
 Do I know why this fix worked? 
If the answer is no, then more learning still needs to happen. 
A useful rule is this: 
Use AI to explain, guide, and debug. Do not use it as a substitute for thinking 
through the code yourself.

Introduction 
Creativity is often misunderstood. 
Some people think creativity is something you either have or do not have. Others

Why Brainstorming Matters 
Many students struggle most at the beginning of a project. 
They may be assigned a topic but have no idea what angle to take. They may need to 
write a story, build a presentation, design an experiment, or create a project, but feel 
stuck because they cannot think of a strong starting point.

Brainstorming is the process of generating many possible ideas before choosing the

For example, a student might ask:

Or:

These kinds of prompts allow students to test possibilities without committing too early. 
They can compare options, borrow one strong element, reject the weaker ones, and 
move forward with a clearer vision.

One of the most valuable uses of AI in ELA is as a writing coach. 
Students often have ideas, but their drafts may need help with clarity, structure, 
transitions, or development. AI can support revision by giving suggestions that the 
student then evaluates and chooses whether to use. 
For example, a student might ask:

===== PAGE 78 =====

meaning. 
Or:

These are strong uses because the student’s own draft remains at the center. The AI is 
helping the writer improve, not replacing the writer. 
This is very different from asking AI to write the introduction, body paragraphs, or 
conclusion. If the tool is doing the composing, then the student is missing the chance to 
build one of the core skills ELA is designed to teach. 
Protecting Voice and Interpretation 
In ELA, student voice matters. 
A good paragraph is not just grammatically correct. It also reflects the student’s 
interpretation, tone, and choices as a writer. If AI rewrites too much, the work may 
sound polished but generic. It may lose what makes the writing feel personal and 
authentic. 
This matters especially in literary analysis. A novel, poem, or speech can often be 
interpreted in more than one way. Students need practice developing their own 
interpretation and supporting it with evidence. AI may suggest possibilities, but the 
student must still decide what they think and why. 
A useful rule is this: Use AI to improve your writing, not to replace your voice. 
Responsible and Irresponsible Uses in ELA 
Responsible uses of AI in ELA include: 
 clarifying difficult passages 
 brainstorming themes, claims, or essay angles 
 generating vocabulary practice or discussion questions 
 getting feedback on clarity, structure, or transitions 
 preparing for class discussion or oral presentation 
Irresponsible uses include: 
 asking AI to write a full essay or paragraph that will be submitted as student 
work 
 using AI summaries instead of reading the assigned text

 copying AI interpretations without understanding or agreeing with them 
 submitting revised text that no longer sounds like the student’s own writing 
The question is not simply whether AI was used. The question is whether the student 
still did the reading, thinking, and writing that the assignment was meant to develop.

brainstorm and revise, but it does not replace her reading or thinking.

 a weak prompt that asks AI to do the writing 
 a stronger prompt that asks for brainstorming help 
 a revision prompt that keeps the student’s draft at the center 
Students can discuss which prompt supports learning most clearly and why. This helps

Chapter 13 — AI in Math 
 
Key Question 
How can students use AI to understand mathematical reasoning, practice more 
effectively, and learn from mistakes without depending on AI for final answers? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI can support explanation, practice, and error analysis in math. 
 Use AI to learn processes step by step instead of collecting answers. 
 Recognize common mistakes and ask better mathematical follow-up questions. 
 Distinguish between responsible support and shortcut learning in math class. 
 Develop stronger habits of checking work, explaining reasoning, and practicing 
independently. 
Introduction 
Math is not just about getting correct answers. It is about noticing patterns, 
understanding relationships, choosing strategies, and explaining why a solution works. 
Those habits make students stronger mathematicians over time. 
AI can help students in math, but it can also be misused very easily. A student can paste 
in a problem and get a finished answer in seconds. That may feel useful in the moment, 
but if the student has not understood the process, very little learning has taken place. 
The best use of AI in math is not answer-getting. It is reasoning support. AI can 
explain a process, break a problem into steps, identify common mistakes, generate 
similar practice problems, and help students reflect on why an approach works. When 
used that way, AI becomes a math coach rather than a shortcut. 
For example, a student may understand the basic idea of slope but freeze when a word 
problem asks them to apply it in a new way. Another student may know how to solve 
one type of equation but become confused when the numbers are arranged differently. 
AI can be especially helpful in those moments because it can slow the process down and 
make the underlying structure easier to see.

===== PAGE 82 =====
Using AI to Understand Steps 
Many students get stuck in math not because the entire topic is impossible, but because 
one step is unclear. AI can be helpful when students need a process explained more 
carefully. 
For example, a student might ask: 
Show me how to solve this equation step by step and explain why each step 
works. 
That is a strong prompt because it focuses on understanding, not just the answer. 
Students can also ask for simpler explanations, alternate methods, or examples with 
smaller numbers first. 
This is especially useful in algebra, geometry, and statistics, where students may know 
some of the pieces but struggle to see how they fit together. AI can make the structure 
of a problem more visible. 
AI for Practice and Pattern Recognition 
One of the best ways to learn math is to practice different versions of the same kind of 
problem. AI can help by creating new examples that match the concept a student is 
trying to learn. 
A student might ask: 
Give me three practice problems like this one and make them get harder each 
time. 
Or: 
Show me two examples of how this type of problem usually works. 
This kind of use is powerful because it builds pattern recognition. Students begin to 
notice that certain problems share structures and strategies. Over time, that helps them 
become more independent problem solvers. 
Using AI to Learn from Mistakes 
Error analysis is one of the most useful math habits students can build. 
Sometimes a student gets a wrong answer but does not know where the mistake 
happened. AI can help identify the likely problem and explain it more clearly. 
For example: 
Check my work and explain where my reasoning went off track. 
Or:

===== PAGE 83 =====
What is one common mistake students make on this kind of problem? 
This is a strong use of AI because students often learn more from corrected mistakes 
than from finished solutions. The student is still involved in the math. The tool is simply 
helping reveal what needs more attention. 
 
What AI Should Not Replace in Math 
AI should not replace the parts of math that help students build reasoning. 
If a student asks AI for the final answer and copies it, the student may finish the 
assignment but will likely struggle the next time a similar problem appears. This is 
especially true on quizzes, tests, and later units that depend on earlier understanding. 
Math learning becomes stronger when students: 
 try the problem first 
 ask for help on the step that is confusing 
 study the logic of the solution 
 practice a similar example independently 
 check whether the answer makes sense 
AI should support that cycle, not bypass it. 
Using AI Responsibly in Math 
In math, responsible use is less about whether AI appears at all and more about what 
the student is still doing. If AI is explaining steps, generating practice, or helping 
locate mistakes, it is supporting learning. If it is replacing the student’s attempt, 
reasoning, or written work, it is weakening the very skill math class is meant to build. 
A good rule is this: If AI solves it for you, ask it to teach you how. Then try a new 
one on your own.

After reading the explanation, Marcus tries a second problem alone. Then he asks:

In this example, AI helps Marcus understand the method and practice it independently. 
That is much more valuable than simply seeing the finished answer once. 
 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can ask students to compare three kinds of math prompts: 
 an answer-only prompt 
 a step-by-step explanation prompt 
 a practice-and-feedback prompt 
Students can discuss which prompt leads to the strongest learning and why. This helps 
them understand that how they ask for help affects what kind of help they receive.

apply the reasoning independently afterward. 
Prompt Example

After using the prompt, ask yourself: 
 Did I understand the reasoning, or did I only follow the steps? 
 Could I solve a similar problem without help? 
 What kind of mistake am I most likely to make next time?

Artificial intelligence can be an extraordinary learning tool. 
It can explain difficult ideas, help students organize their thinking, generate practice 
questions, and support writing, research, coding, and creativity. Used well, it can 
strengthen learning and make schoolwork more interactive and engaging. 
But powerful tools always raise important questions. 
When is it acceptable to use AI for schoolwork? 
When does support become dishonesty? 
How should students respond when AI gives incorrect or biased information? 
What does responsible use actually look like in everyday practice? 
These questions matter because education is not only about getting work done. It is 
about building skills, judgment, habits, and integrity. A student who uses AI to avoid 
thinking may finish faster, but that student loses the opportunity to grow. A student 
who uses AI to deepen understanding, check reasoning, and improve their work is using 
the technology in a very different—and much more valuable—way. 
This chapter is about making that difference clear.

===== PAGE 103 =====
Responsibility Begins With Purpose 
The first question students should ask when using AI is simple: 
Why am I using this tool? 
That question matters because the same AI feature can be used responsibly in one 
situation and irresponsibly in another. 
For example, asking AI to explain a difficult math concept can be a strong use of the 
tool. Asking AI to complete a graded assignment that is supposed to measure your own 
understanding is something very different. 
Purpose changes everything. 
Students who use AI responsibly are usually trying to do one or more of the following: 
 understand a concept more clearly 
 generate practice questions 
 organize ideas 
 improve a draft 
 review for a test 
 explore different perspectives 
 get help with the next step in a process 
In each of those cases, the student is still doing the learning. 
By contrast, students who use AI irresponsibly often use it to avoid effort rather than 
support growth. They may ask for finished essays, completed homework answers, or 
work they could not explain themselves. 
The difference is not whether AI is involved. The difference is whether the student is 
still doing the thinking.

===== PAGE 104 =====
Academic Integrity and AI 
Schools have always expected students to do their own work honestly. 
That expectation does not disappear just because new technology exists. 
Academic integrity means being truthful about what work is yours, what ideas came 
from others, and what kind of help you used. In the past, that meant not copying from 
another student, not using unauthorized notes during a test, and not presenting 
someone else’s writing as your own. In the age of AI, the same principle still applies. 
If a student uses AI to understand a concept, brainstorm a topic, or revise a paragraph 
while still doing the work themselves, that may be acceptable depending on the teacher’s 
expectations. If a student uses AI to generate the actual assignment and turns it in as 
their own work, that is a much more serious issue. 
Students should remember this simple rule: 
If you could not explain, defend, or recreate the work yourself, it probably does 
not truly represent your learning. 
That is why honesty matters. Teachers are not only grading a final product. They are 
evaluating understanding, effort, and growth. 
Different Classes May Have Different Rules 
One reason students can feel confused about AI is that not every class will treat it the 
same way. 
A teacher may encourage AI for brainstorming in English but not allow it for a take-
home essay. A science teacher may allow AI to help explain concepts but not to 
complete lab analysis. A history teacher may permit AI-generated practice questions but 
not AI-written research responses. 
This means students should not assume one rule applies everywhere. 
Instead, students should ask: 
 Is AI allowed for this assignment? 
 If so, what kinds of use are acceptable? 
 Should I tell my teacher how I used it? 
 Is the goal of this assignment practice, process, or final demonstration? 
Responsible AI use includes paying attention to those differences. 
A student who asks for clarity before using AI is demonstrating maturity. A student who 
assumes anything is acceptable because the technology is available is more likely to 
create problems for themselves.

===== PAGE 105 =====
Verifying AI-Generated Information 
Responsibility also includes checking whether AI responses are accurate. 
As you learned earlier in the book, AI systems can generate incorrect information that 
sounds polished and believable. This can include false facts, made-up details, inaccurate 
summaries, and invented sources. 
That is why students should never assume that an AI response is correct simply because 
it sounds confident. 
Responsible use means verifying important information through: 
 textbooks 
 teacher materials 
 class notes 
 trusted educational sites 
 library or research sources 
This is especially important for: 
 research projects 
 historical information 
 scientific explanations 
 citations and references 
 statistics and factual claims 
A useful habit is to treat AI as a starting point for understanding, not the final word. 
Bias, Fairness, and Perspective 
Another important part of responsible AI use is recognizing that AI systems are not 
neutral in every situation. 
Because they are trained on human-created data, AI systems can sometimes reflect bias, 
incomplete perspectives, or oversimplified views of people and events. This does not 
always happen in obvious ways. Sometimes the issue is not that the AI gives a 
completely wrong answer, but that it leaves out important context or presents one 
perspective more strongly than another. 
Students should be especially thoughtful when using AI for topics involving: 
 history 
 politics 
 culture 
 identity 
 ethics 
 current events

===== PAGE 106 =====
When a topic has multiple perspectives, responsible AI use means asking broader 
questions, comparing responses, and checking additional sources rather than accepting 
one answer too quickly. 
This is another reason human judgment matters so much. AI can help students explore 
ideas, but students must still evaluate fairness, accuracy, and perspective. 
Privacy and Personal Information 
Responsibility also includes being careful about what information students share with AI 
tools. 
Students should avoid entering: 
 passwords 
 private school account information 
 personal identifying details 
 sensitive health information 
 confidential school documents 
 other students’ private information 
Even when an AI tool feels conversational, it is still a digital system. Students should use 
common sense and protect their privacy just as they would online in any other context. 
A good rule is: 
If you would not post it publicly or share it with a stranger, do not put it into an 
AI tool. 
That habit protects students and helps them build strong digital citizenship skills.

===== PAGE 107 =====
Using AI to Strengthen Learning 
The most responsible use of AI is the kind that clearly strengthens learning. 
Students are usually on solid ground when they use AI to: 
 ask for clearer explanations 
 generate practice questions 
 check understanding 
 compare perspectives 
 organize notes 
 improve the clarity of their own writing 
 break down a large task into smaller steps 
These uses support learning while keeping the student actively involved. 
Students move into less responsible territory when they use AI to: 
 write graded work for them 
 complete problem sets without understanding 
 produce answers they cannot explain 
 bypass effort rather than support it 
This distinction should guide every decision. 
Student Example 
A student named Daniel is assigned a short history response about the causes of the 
Civil War. 
He is not sure how to begin, so he asks AI: 
Help me understand the main causes of the Civil War in simple terms. 
After reading the explanation, Daniel checks his class notes and textbook. Then he asks: 
Help me organize these ideas into a short outline for a paragraph response. 
Using that outline, Daniel writes the paragraph himself. 
This is a responsible use of AI. The tool helped him understand the topic and organize 
his thinking, but the final work still reflected his own understanding. 
Now imagine a different version. Daniel asks AI to write the full paragraph and turns it 
in without revising or understanding it. That is a very different situation. 
The tool may be the same, but the use is not.

===== PAGE 108 =====
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can use this chapter to lead a discussion about what responsible AI use looks 
like in real classroom situations. 
For example, students can be given sample scenarios and asked to decide whether each 
one reflects responsible or irresponsible use. 
Examples might include: 
 using AI to generate quiz questions before a test 
 asking AI to write a final essay draft 
 using AI to explain a difficult equation 
 copying AI-generated code without understanding it 
 using AI to organize notes into a study guide 
This kind of discussion helps students move beyond vague ideas about “good” or “bad” 
use and instead think more clearly about purpose, honesty, and learning. 
Try This 
Open an AI tool and ask: 
Prompt Example

 Would using this explanation help me learn? 
 What parts should I verify with other sources? 
 How would I use this responsibly for a class assignment? 
 Now compare that with this prompt: 
Prompt Example

What is the difference in purpose between the two prompts? Which one supports 
learning, and which one avoids it?

Chapter 18 — AI, Media Literacy, and What to 
Trust 
 
Key Question 
How can students tell what to trust in a world where AI can generate realistic text, 
images, audio, and video that may be misleading or false? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI can be used to create misinformation and manipulated 
media. 
 Recognize why believable content is not always trustworthy content. 
 Learn practical habits for checking claims, images, summaries, and viral 
posts. 
 Distinguish between healthy skepticism and automatic cynicism. 
 Use media literacy skills to verify what is real before sharing, trusting, or 
repeating it. 
Introduction 
Students today live in a media environment where information moves fast and attention 
is valuable. A post, image, video clip, or summary can spread across social platforms in 
minutes. In that environment, artificial intelligence makes both helpful and harmful 
things easier to create. AI can help people explain ideas, make visuals, and communicate 
more quickly. But it can also be used to generate misinformation, manipulate media, and 
create content that looks real even when it is false or misleading. That means students 
need a skill that goes beyond simply using AI tools: they need to know how to evaluate 
what they see and decide what deserves trust. 
AI media literacy means learning how to pause, question, verify, and think critically 
when digital content looks convincing. It includes checking sources, comparing claims, 
questioning motives, and recognizing that even realistic-looking images, videos, and 
summaries may not tell the truth. In a world shaped by synthetic media, strong students 
do not trust content just because it is polished. They ask better questions first.

===== PAGE 111 =====
What AI-Generated Misinformation Looks Like 
Misinformation is false or misleading information that spreads as if it were true. 
Sometimes it is shared by people who do not realize it is wrong. Other times it is created 
or reshaped on purpose to influence, confuse, embarrass, or manipulate others. 
AI makes misinformation easier to produce because it can generate believable content 
quickly and at scale. That may include: 
 articles or posts that sound factual but contain false claims 
 images that look like real photographs but were generated or altered 
 audio clips that imitate a person’s voice 
 videos that make it appear someone said or did something that never 
happened 
 false summaries that leave out key context or distort meaning 
This matters because students are often surrounded by fast-moving media, and many 
people judge trustworthiness by how polished something looks. AI challenges that habit. 
In an AI-rich world, realism is no longer enough. 
Deepfakes and Synthetic Media 
One of the clearest examples of AI-powered misinformation is the deepfake. 
A deepfake is manipulated or synthetic media—usually video, image, or audio—that 
uses AI to imitate a real person’s appearance or voice. Some deepfakes are made for 
entertainment or parody, but others are designed to deceive. They may try to damage a 
reputation, create confusion, influence public opinion, or spread false narratives online. 
Because they can be highly convincing, widely shareable, and easier to produce than 
older forms of digital editing, deepfakes challenge one of the oldest habits of media 
trust: the assumption that seeing is believing. 
Students do not need to become forensic analysts to respond wisely. But they do need 
to understand an important idea: if seeing is no longer always believing, then trust must 
come from verification, not instinct. UNESCO argues that in a world shaped by 
synthetic media, education must help students rebuild how they establish truth rather 
than rely only on surface realism.

===== PAGE 112 =====
False Summaries and Confident Errors 
Not all AI misinformation looks dramatic. Some of it looks ordinary and helpful. 
A student might read an AI-generated summary of an article, video, or event and assume 
it captures the truth. But summaries can be misleading when they leave out context, 
flatten disagreement, exaggerate one point, or quietly include incorrect details. This is 
especially risky when students are already busy and tempted to trust the quick version. 
For example, an AI summary of a current event might sound clear and balanced while 
leaving out who created the original claim, whether reliable reporting confirmed it, or 
whether important evidence is still uncertain. This is why a summary should be treated 
as a starting point, not a final authority. 
Manipulation on Social Platforms 
Social platforms reward content that grabs attention quickly. That means posts that are 
emotional, shocking, or visually dramatic often spread faster than careful explanations. 
AI can intensify this problem by making it easier to generate attention-grabbing visuals, 
fake quotes, misleading captions, and viral-looking media. 
Students should be especially careful when a post seems designed to make them react 
before they think. A useful habit is to ask: 
 Who created this? 
 What do they want me to believe or do? 
 What evidence supports the claim? 
 Has a trusted source confirmed it? 
 Why might this be spreading right now? 
These questions do not make students cynical. They make students careful. 
How to Verify What Is Real 
The strongest response to AI misinformation is not guessing. It is verification. That 
means slowing down long enough to compare claims, check sources, and ask what 
evidence actually supports what is being shown or said. 
Media literacy experts increasingly recommend a few practical habits. Students can 
compare a claim across multiple reliable sources, use reverse-image search to see where a 
picture has appeared before, and check whether reputable reporting has confirmed the 
same story. Educational guidance on AI media literacy emphasizes that systematic 
verification works better than trusting intuition alone. SchoolAI recommends teaching 
students to slow down, observe details, and use practical verification tools rather than 
relying on instinct, while MIT frames media literacy as the set of skills students need to

===== PAGE 113 =====
locate, interpret, and evaluate media in a world shaped by deepfakes and 
misinformation. 
Useful verification habits include: 
 cross-checking a claim with trusted news or educational sources 
 using reverse-image search when a photo feels suspicious or appears out of 
context 
 looking for missing context, dates, authorship, or source evidence 
 noticing whether a post is trying to rush your reaction 
 reading laterally by opening other sources instead of staying on one page or 
post 
A strong rule is this: Do not ask only “Does this look real?” Ask “What evidence 
shows this is real?” 
Healthy Skepticism vs. Automatic Distrust 
Students should not respond to AI media by assuming everything is fake. That is not 
media literacy. It is confusion. 
Healthy skepticism means pausing long enough to ask what evidence supports a claim. It 
means being willing to verify before trusting or sharing. Automatic distrust means 
assuming nothing can be known, which is also unhelpful. 
The goal is not to panic or become suspicious of everything online. The goal is to 
become more thoughtful about how truth is established. In practice, that means trusting 
evidence, credible sourcing, and careful verification more than appearance, speed, or 
popularity. 
Student Example 
A student named Maya sees a short video clip spreading online that appears to show a 
public figure saying something outrageous. The clip is dramatic, and many people in the 
comments are reacting as if it must be true. 
Instead of reposting it right away, Maya pauses. She searches to see whether trusted 
news sources reported the same statement. She also notices that the lip movements in 
one moment seem slightly off and that the video is being shared from accounts she does 
not recognize. After checking, she discovers that the clip was edited and presented 
without the original context. 
In this example, Maya does not need perfect technical knowledge. What helps her most 
is the habit of slowing down, checking for confirmation, and refusing to trust virality by 
itself.

===== PAGE 114 =====
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can show students a mix of media examples such as a real news image, an AI-
generated image, a misleading caption, and a short summary of a current event. Students 
can work in groups to decide what questions they would ask before trusting or sharing 
each one. 
The class can discuss: 
 What evidence is visible and what evidence is missing? 
 Which item is easiest to trust too quickly? 
 What verification step would help most here? 
 Why do some posts spread faster than careful fact-checks? 
This helps students see that media literacy is not about memorizing one trick. It is about 
building a repeatable way of thinking. 
Try This 
Prompt Example

misleading. Give me five questions I should ask before trusting or sharing it, and

After reading the response, ask yourself: 
 What evidence would actually help confirm this claim? 
 Am I trusting this because it is popular, emotional, or visually convincing? 
 What source or verification step would help me decide more carefully?

Chapter 19 — AI and the Future of Work 
 
Key Question 
How is artificial intelligence changing jobs, careers, and the skills students will need in 
the future? 
Learning Objectives 
 Understand how AI is affecting the world of work 
 Learn why AI literacy is becoming an important future skill 
 Recognize which human skills remain especially valuable in an AI-driven world 
 Begin thinking about how students can prepare for careers shaped by AI 
Introduction 
Artificial intelligence is not only changing how students learn. It is also changing how 
adults work. 
Across many industries, AI tools are being used to analyze information, draft content, 
automate repetitive tasks, and support decision-making. Businesses use AI to organize 
data, customer service teams use it to respond faster, designers use it to explore ideas, 
and programmers use it to write and test code more efficiently. Even fields that once 
seemed far removed from technology—such as healthcare, law, education, and the 
arts—are being reshaped by AI in different ways. 
This can make the future feel exciting, but it can also make it feel uncertain. 
Students may wonder: 
 Will AI replace jobs? 
 What careers will still matter? 
 What skills should I be developing now? 
 How can I prepare for a future that keeps changing? 
These are important questions. The goal of this chapter is not to predict every job of the 
future. Instead, it is to help students understand a more useful idea: 
The future will not belong to people who ignore AI. It will belong to people who 
learn how to work with it wisely.

===== PAGE 117 =====
AI Is Changing Work, Not Ending Work 
One of the biggest misunderstandings about AI is the belief that it will simply “take all 
the jobs.” 
The truth is more complicated. 
AI is changing how work gets done, especially in jobs that involve repetitive tasks, large 
amounts of information, or routine communication. Some tasks may shrink, disappear, 
or require fewer people than before. Some roles may change so much that workers will 
need to adapt in major ways. At the same time, new tools, responsibilities, and career 
paths are also emerging. 
History shows a similar pattern with other technologies. Calculators changed how 
people approached arithmetic, but they did not eliminate math. Computers changed 
office work, but they also created entire new industries. The internet changed 
communication, research, and business, but it also created jobs that would have been 
impossible to imagine a generation earlier. 
AI is likely to do the same. 
Rather than ending work, AI is changing the kinds of work people do and the skills that 
matter most. 
Jobs Already Affected by AI 
AI is already influencing many fields that students may enter in the future. 
In healthcare, AI tools can help analyze medical images and assist with diagnosis. 
In business, AI can summarize reports, identify trends, and support planning. 
In education, AI can generate practice materials and help personalize instruction. 
In technology, AI can support coding, testing, and design. 
In media and marketing, AI can assist with content creation, audience analysis, and 
creative brainstorming. 
This does not mean the AI does the whole job. 
What it usually means is that people in those jobs are increasingly expected to 
understand how to use AI tools as part of their work. That is why AI literacy matters. 
The ability to use AI effectively may become as common and important as using email, 
spreadsheets, or search engines.

===== PAGE 118 =====
The Skills That Will Matter More 
As AI becomes more capable, some human skills become even more important. 
Students often assume that if AI can generate writing, solve problems, or answer 
questions, then human thinking matters less. In reality, the opposite is often true. 
When information becomes easier to generate, it becomes more important to evaluate, 
interpret, and apply that information wisely. 
That means the future will still strongly reward skills such as: 
 critical thinking 
 communication 
 creativity 
 collaboration 
 ethical judgment 
 adaptability 
 problem solving 
AI can help produce information quickly, but it cannot replace human responsibility, 
empathy, or wisdom. It cannot fully replace the value of asking the right question, 
choosing between competing ideas, or making a thoughtful decision in a complex 
situation. 
Students who develop these human strengths alongside AI literacy will be much better 
prepared for future careers than students who only learn how to use tools without 
learning how to think. 
AI Literacy as a Career Skill 
Just as digital literacy became essential in the internet age, AI literacy is becoming 
increasingly important in the AI age. 
AI literacy does not mean students need to become software engineers or machine 
learning experts. It means they should know enough to: 
 understand what AI can and cannot do 
 use AI tools effectively 
 write clear prompts 
 verify AI-generated information 
 think critically about outputs 
 use AI responsibly in school and work 
These are practical skills. 
A student who knows how to use AI to organize ideas, improve communication, 
research topics, and solve problems will likely have an advantage over someone who 
either avoids AI completely or uses it carelessly.

===== PAGE 119 =====
The goal is not to become dependent on AI. The goal is to become capable in a world 
where AI is part of daily work. 
AI Will Affect Different Careers in Different Ways 
Not every career will be changed in exactly the same way. 
Some jobs may rely heavily on AI tools for efficiency. Others may use AI only 
occasionally. Some fields will emphasize technical AI skills more than others, while 
many jobs will still depend mostly on communication, judgment, and human interaction. 
For example: 
A teacher may use AI to generate practice materials or simplify lesson preparation. 
A doctor may use AI to help review patterns in medical data. 
A lawyer may use AI to summarize documents quickly. 
A designer may use AI to generate visual possibilities. 
A software developer may use AI to debug code and suggest structure. 
A business professional may use AI to analyze trends and draft reports. 
In each case, the work still depends on a person making decisions, evaluating outcomes, 
and taking responsibility for the final result. 
That is why students should not focus only on learning one specific AI tool. Tools will 
change. What matters more is learning how to think, adapt, and use technology 
intelligently. 
Students Can Use AI to Explore Careers 
AI can also help students think about their future more actively. 
A student who is unsure what careers fit their interests can use AI to explore 
possibilities. For example, they might ask: 
What careers combine technology and creativity? 
Or: 
What skills do I need if I want to become a software engineer? 
Or: 
What jobs are likely to use artificial intelligence in the future? 
These kinds of prompts can help students begin asking better career questions. AI can 
provide a starting point, but students should still talk to teachers, counselors, family 
members, and professionals when making important decisions. 
AI is helpful for exploration. It is not a substitute for real guidance and reflection.

Key Question 
What kinds of prompts help students get the most useful, responsible, and 
educational results from AI? 
Learning Objectives 
 Recognize what makes a prompt useful for students 
 Learn prompt patterns that support studying, writing, research, and creativity 
 Use prompts to deepen learning rather than avoid it

Introduction 
The quality of AI responses depends heavily on the quality of the prompts 
students use. A vague prompt often produces a vague answer, while a thoughtful 
prompt can lead to a clearer explanation, better examples, stronger feedback, and more 
useful support. This matters because AI is not just a tool for getting information. It is a 
tool for asking better questions. 
Students who learn how to write strong prompts gain a real advantage. They can study 
more effectively, organize ideas more clearly, explore topics more deeply, and use AI in 
ways that support learning rather than replace it. This chapter gathers some of the most 
useful prompts in the entire book and organizes them into practical categories, not as 
rigid scripts to memorize, but as a toolkit students can adapt to many situations. 
The best way to use this chapter is not to memorize every prompt exactly. Instead, pay 
attention to the structure of the prompts and the kinds of support they create. Over 
time, students should learn to shape these prompts to fit their own classes, projects, and 
learning goals, just as they would adapt any other academic strategy. 
What Makes a Prompt Powerful? 
A powerful prompt usually does at least one of the following: 
 gives the AI a clear task 
 explains the intended audience or level 
 asks for a useful format 
 encourages explanation instead of just answers 
 creates opportunities for practice or feedback

In other words, strong prompts guide the AI toward being helpful in a specific way. 
For example, compare these two prompts: 
Weak prompt: 
Explain photosynthesis. 
Stronger prompt:

What an AI-Empowered Learner Looks Like 
An AI-empowered learner does not use AI passively. Instead of asking the tool to do all

Curiosity Still Matters Most 
One of the greatest mistakes students can make is thinking that AI replaces the need for 
curiosity. 
In reality, curiosity becomes even more important in the age of AI. 
AI can generate answers quickly, but it does not decide which questions matter most. It 
does not decide what is worth exploring more deeply. It does not replace the spark that 
leads a student to ask “why,” “what if,” or “how does this connect to something else?” 
Curiosity is what drives learning forward. 
A curious student might begin with a basic question, then ask follow-up questions, look

Judgment Matters More in an AI World

weaker judgment.

questioned.

polished. Instead, that student asks: 
 Does this make sense? 
 Is this accurate? 
 Does this match what I know from class? 
 What might be missing here? 
 What should I verify?

Judgment is what helps students separate speed from truth, convenience from

The stronger the tool becomes, the more important human judgment becomes too.

Appendix A — Using AI to Build With AI 
Purpose of This Appendix 
This appendix shows students how to use AI for a different kind of work: not just 
answering questions, but helping plan, design, build, test, and improve a project. The 
goal is to introduce a practical workflow for using AI across multiple stages of creation 
while keeping the student in charge of the thinking, decisions, and final choices. 
In the chapters of this book, you explored how AI can support studying, research, 
writing, problem solving, coding, and organization. This appendix builds on those ideas 
by showing how AI can also support project development. That means using AI to 
help move from a rough idea to a simple finished product through a structured sequence 
of steps. 
The focus here is not on building a complex commercial product. The focus is on 
learning a repeatable process students can use when they want to create something 
simple, improve an idea, or explore how builders and developers think. Even if a student 
never becomes a programmer, this appendix teaches useful habits of planning, iteration, 
and critical evaluation. 
The Big Idea: AI as a Project Team 
One of the most powerful ways to use AI is to stop thinking of it as a single all-purpose 
answer machine. Instead, it can be more useful to think of AI as a project team that 
helps with different kinds of work at different times. 
For example, when building even a simple project, you may need help with: 
 defining the goal clearly 
 deciding what features matter most 
 planning the structure of the solution 
 writing or organizing the first draft of the work 
 testing what works and what does not 
 revising the project based on feedback 
When students use AI well, they can prompt the tool to help with each of these roles 
one at a time. This approach is sometimes called AI orchestration because the student 
is directing the workflow, deciding what happens next, and using AI support in a 
deliberate sequence rather than in one giant request. 
A useful principle to remember is this: 
Advanced AI use is not about getting one perfect response. It is about guiding a 
process, evaluating each stage, and improving the result step by step.

===== PAGE 142 =====
A Student-Friendly Example Project 
To make this process concrete, this appendix uses a simple sample project: an AI Study 
Quiz Generator. The idea is straightforward. A student enters a topic they want to 
review, and the project displays a small set of quiz questions for practice. 
This kind of project works well as an example because it connects directly to the rest of 
the book. It also shows how AI can support something educational, manageable, and 
realistic for students to imagine building. 
A simple version of the project might work like this: 
 The user enters a topic such as photosynthesis, the Civil War, or quadratic 
equations. 
 The system displays a short set of practice questions. 
 The student uses the questions to review and check understanding. 
In a classroom demo or beginner project, the quiz questions might be prewritten or 
simulated. In a more advanced version, a live AI tool might help generate the questions. 
Either way, the learning value comes from understanding the project workflow. 
The Five Core Roles in the Workflow 
To keep the workflow simple and useful, students can think in terms of five core roles. 
These roles are not jobs students must memorize. They are just a helpful way to 
organize what kind of help AI is providing at each stage. 
The Student or Builder 
The student begins the process by identifying the problem, defining the goal, and 
deciding what kind of project is worth building. AI can help clarify the idea, but the 
student is still responsible for choosing the direction. 
The Planner 
This role helps define the purpose of the project, the audience, and the most important 
features. Students can ask AI to act like a planner or product manager and help answer 
questions such as: What problem does this solve? What is the simplest useful version? 
What should happen first? 
The Designer 
This role helps map out the structure of the project. In a coding project, that may mean 
deciding what screens, components, or steps are needed. In another kind of project, it 
may mean outlining the process, sequence, or layout.

===== PAGE 143 =====
The Builder 
This role helps generate the first workable version of the project. Depending on the 
task, that might mean writing code, drafting text, creating a checklist, building slides, or 
producing a rough prototype. 
The Tester and Reviewer 
This role checks whether the project actually works, whether the instructions are clear, 
whether anything is missing, and what should be improved next. Testing is one of the 
most important stages because it turns a rough draft into something more usable and 
thoughtful. 
A Step-by-Step AI Build Workflow 
Students can use the following sequence when trying to build a small project with AI 
support. This process works for digital products, presentations, websites, prototypes, or 
other structured creations. 
Step 1 — Define the Goal Clearly 
Start by naming the problem you want to solve or the thing you want to create. The 
clearer the goal, the more useful the AI support will be. 
Prompt example: I want to build a simple study tool that helps students review a topic 
using a short quiz. Help me define the goal clearly and identify the most important 
feature. 
Step 2 — Decide What the Simplest Useful Version Looks 
Like 
Before trying to build everything, decide what the smallest version of the project should 
do. This is often called an MVP, or minimum viable product. In student-friendly terms, 
it means: What is the simplest version that still proves the idea works? 
Prompt example: Act like a planner. What is the simplest useful version of a study quiz 
generator for students, and what should it do first? 
Step 3 — Outline the Project Structure 
Next, ask AI to help map out the major parts. For a website, that may include an input 
area, a button, and a place to show results. For another kind of project, it may mean an 
introduction, process, output, and reflection section. 
Prompt example: Act like a designer. Outline the main parts of this project and explain 
how they should work together in a simple way.

===== PAGE 144 =====
Step 4 — Build a First Draft or Prototype 
Once the structure is clear, AI can help generate the first version. If the project involves 
code, this may be starter code. If it involves a presentation or plan, it may be a draft 
outline or rough content. The goal at this stage is not perfection. The goal is to create 
something workable that can be improved. 
This step helps turn a rough idea into a clear product concept. 
Prompt example: Act like a builder. Create a simple first version of this project that I 
can review and improve. 
Step 5 — Test What Works and What Does Not 
Good builders test early. Students should ask whether the project is clear, whether the 
instructions make sense, whether parts are missing, and whether the result actually 
solves the original problem. 
Prompt example: Act like a tester. Review this project and point out anything 
confusing, missing, or likely to cause problems. 
Step 6 — Revise and Improve 
After testing, use AI to help improve the project one issue at a time. Students should 
avoid asking for a total rewrite too quickly. It is usually better to make focused 
improvements so they can understand what changed and why. 
Prompt example: Improve this project by making the instructions clearer, the layout 
easier to follow, and the final result more useful for students. 
Practical Rules for Students Using AI to Build 
 Start small. Build the simplest useful version before adding extras. 
 Stay in charge. AI can suggest, draft, and organize, but the student should still 
decide what belongs in the final project. 
 Check accuracy. If AI generates facts, instructions, or code explanations, 
verify what matters before trusting it. 
 Revise in stages. Improve one part at a time rather than asking AI to redo 
everything at once. 
 Understand what you built. If you cannot explain how your own project 
works, more learning still needs to happen.

===== PAGE 145 =====
Common Mistakes to Avoid 
 Trying to build something too large before the simple version works 
 Accepting AI output without checking whether it matches the goal 
 Letting AI make all the design decisions without student judgment 
 Using code or project content that the student does not understand 
 Skipping testing and assuming the first version is good enough 
Reflection Questions 
After using AI to build or improve a project, students should pause and ask: 
 Did AI help me think more clearly, or did I let it do too much of the 
thinking? 
 Which stage of the workflow helped most? 
 What part of the final result still reflects my own judgment and choices? 
 What would I improve if I built the project again? 
 What did I learn about planning, testing, or revising through this process?

• ask questions about topics they are studying 
• summarize information or compare viewpoints 
• generate creative ideas, images, or starting points for research 
One feature that makes Grok different from some other AI tools is its connection to 
real-time information and conversations occurring online. This can make it helpful when 
a student wants to understand what people are discussing about a current issue. 
However, current information is not always reliable information. Social media posts may 
be incomplete, biased, misleading, or false. Students using Grok should carefully verify 
important claims with trusted sources, especially when researching news, science, 
history, or controversial topics. In a school setting, students should also use only 
teacher-approved or district-approved AI tools and follow classroom rules for 
appropriate use.

AI Research Tools 
Research tools are different from chat assistants because they are more focused on 
helping users locate, organize, and verify information. 
These tools can be especially useful when students are: 
 starting a research paper 
 exploring a historical topic 
 gathering source-based information 
 comparing multiple sources 
 checking claims or facts 
Examples include: 
 Perplexity 
 Elicit 
 NotebookLM 
These tools are often more useful when students need information connected to sources 
rather than a simple conversational explanation. 
Perplexity 
Perplexity is especially helpful for research because it often combines AI-generated 
responses with source links. This makes it easier for students to move from an 
explanation to actual supporting material. 
Students can use Perplexity to: 
 research current or historical topics 
 gather source-based overviews 
 identify useful articles or references 
 compare different explanations 
 verify claims more easily 
This makes it a particularly strong tool for projects, essays, and presentations where 
source awareness matters.

===== PAGE 151 =====
Elicit 
Elicit is more research-oriented and may be especially useful for students or advanced 
learners who want help finding academic-style information or summarizing research 
sources. It is less of a general chat assistant and more of a research helper. 
Students may use Elicit to: 
 explore research questions 
 summarize article findings 
 compare sources 
 identify themes in research material 
It may be more advanced than what some high school students need every day, but it is 
worth knowing because it introduces a more research-centered kind of AI tool. 
NotebookLM 
NotebookLM is especially useful because it allows students to work with their own 
materials. Instead of only asking general questions, students can upload notes, readings, 
or documents and then ask questions based on those materials. 
That makes it particularly useful for: 
 studying class notes 
 reviewing readings 
 preparing for tests 
 organizing project materials 
 generating summaries from specific content 
A student who wants to study from class materials rather than from the broader internet 
may find NotebookLM especially helpful.

===== PAGE 152 =====
AI Writing Tools 
Some AI tools are designed specifically to help with writing, editing, and organization. 
These tools are often best used after a student has already started writing. They can 
help improve clarity, grammar, structure, and tone, but they should not replace the 
student’s own ideas and voice. 
Examples include: 
 Grammarly 
 Notion AI 
Grammarly 
Grammarly is useful for helping students improve grammar, sentence clarity, and writing 
flow. It is less about generating ideas from scratch and more about polishing work that 
already exists. 
Students can use Grammarly to: 
 correct grammar issues 
 improve sentence structure 
 strengthen word choice 
 reduce awkward phrasing 
 make writing more clear and readable 
It is especially useful during revision, but students should still decide which suggestions 
actually improve their writing. 
Notion AI 
Notion AI is useful for students who want help organizing information, notes, tasks, 
and writing ideas in one place. Because Notion is built around planning and structured 
organization, its AI features can support both productivity and writing. 
Students can use Notion AI to: 
 summarize notes 
 generate outlines 
 organize project ideas 
 build study systems 
 track tasks and deadlines 
It can be especially useful for students who like keeping schoolwork, project planning, 
and writing support in one system.

Canva AI 
Canva is especially useful because it combines design tools with AI-assisted features.

Students can use Canva to:

AI Coding Tools 
Some AI tools are specifically useful for programming and coding support. 
These tools are best when students need help with: 
 explaining code 
 debugging 
 exploring programming ideas 
 learning syntax 
 testing logic 
Examples include: 
 GitHub Copilot 
 Codeium 
 general AI chat tools used carefully for code explanation 
These tools can be valuable, but students should use them to learn rather than simply 
copy solutions. 
GitHub Copilot 
GitHub Copilot is designed to assist with coding by suggesting code and helping 
programmers work more efficiently. For students, it can be useful as a support tool 
when learning programming concepts or building small projects. 
Used responsibly, Copilot can help students: 
 understand common coding patterns 
 get suggestions while coding 
 debug small issues 
 move through repetitive parts of programming more efficiently

===== PAGE 155 =====
Used poorly, however, it can make students overdependent. That is why it should be 
treated as a coach, not as a replacement for understanding. 
Choosing the Right Tool 
One of the most important AI skills students can develop is learning how to choose the 
right tool for the task. 
A student who needs help understanding a difficult topic may want an AI chat assistant. 
A student beginning a research project may want a research-focused tool. 
A student revising an essay may want a writing assistant. 
A student creating a presentation may want a design-oriented tool. 
A student learning Python may want a coding support tool. 
This is why the book includes diagrams such as The AI Landscape and Choosing the 
Right AI Tool. They help students see that “AI” is not just one thing. It is an 
ecosystem of different tools with different strengths. 
Students do not need to master every tool. They do need to become thoughtful about 
choosing the right one. 
Student Example 
A student named Ethan is working on three different tasks in the same week: 
 a history research project 
 a persuasive essay 
 a science presentation 
Instead of using the same AI tool in the same way for every task, Ethan chooses based 
on the need. 
For the history project, he uses a research-oriented tool to explore source-based 
background information. 
For the essay, he uses a writing tool to improve clarity in his draft. 
For the science presentation, he uses a design tool to help create clearer visuals. 
This is a good example of AI literacy in action. Ethan is not just “using AI.” He is 
choosing tools intentionally.

Classroom Idea 
Teachers can use this section to help students compare AI tool categories rather than 
focusing only on brand names. 
For example, a class might discuss: 
 Which tool is best for studying? 
 Which is best for research? 
 Which is best for writing support? 
 Which is best for images or presentations? 
 Which is best for coding help? 
This helps students think in terms of function and purpose, which is a more durable 
skill than simply memorizing which tools are popular right now. 
Responsible Use Reminder 
No matter which AI tool students choose, the same principles still apply. 
Students should use AI to: 
 support learning 
 clarify understanding 
 improve organization 
 strengthen communication 
 explore ideas more deeply 
Students should not use AI to: 
 avoid thinking 
 submit work they do not understand 
 bypass class expectations 
 trust unverified information automatically 
The tool matters, but the mindset matters more. 
Why This Section Matters 
Students are likely to encounter many different AI tools in the years ahead. Some will 
become more powerful. Others will disappear and be replaced. The names may change, 
but the need for judgment will remain. That is why this section matters: it helps students 
understand the AI landscape in practical terms and prepares them to make better 
choices about which tools to use, when to use them, and how to use them responsibly.

Glossary of AI Terms 
How to use this glossary 
Students do not need to memorize every definition in this section. 
Instead, this glossary is meant to be a reference tool. Readers can return to it 
whenever they encounter a word in the book that feels unfamiliar or when they 
want a quick reminder of a key concept. 
Teachers may also find it useful when introducing vocabulary or reviewing 
important ideas related to AI literacy. 
Algorithm 
A set of instructions a computer follows to solve a problem or complete a task. 
Algorithms are the step-by-step logic behind many digital tools, including AI systems. 
Artificial Intelligence (AI) 
Computer systems designed to perform tasks that usually require human intelligence, 
such as understanding language, recognizing patterns, generating ideas, or making 
predictions. 
AI Assistant 
A digital tool that uses artificial intelligence to help users complete tasks such as 
answering questions, explaining concepts, organizing information, or generating content. 
AI Literacy 
The ability to understand what AI is, how it works at a basic level, how to use it 
responsibly, and how to evaluate the quality of its output. 
Automation 
Using technology to complete tasks automatically with less direct human effort. AI often 
increases automation by helping computers handle more complex tasks. 
Bias 
A tendency toward unfairness, imbalance, or incomplete representation. In AI, bias can 
appear when a system reflects patterns in its training data that lead to unfair or 
inaccurate results.

===== PAGE 158 =====
Chatbot 
A computer program designed to simulate conversation with a user through text or 
voice. Many modern AI chatbots are powered by large language models. 
Collaboration 
Working together to complete a task or solve a problem. In the context of this book, 
students can think of AI as a collaborative tool, but not a replacement for human effort 
or teamwork. 
Concept Map 
A visual diagram that shows how ideas connect to one another. Concept maps help 
students organize information and see relationships between topics. 
Context 
The background information included in a prompt or task that helps AI produce a more 
useful response. Context makes prompts clearer and more specific. 
Critical Evaluation 
The process of carefully examining information before accepting it as accurate or useful. 
This is especially important when using AI-generated content. 
Critical Thinking 
The habit of analyzing ideas carefully, asking good questions, checking evidence, and 
making thoughtful judgments instead of accepting information too quickly. 
Data 
Information used by computers to process tasks, recognize patterns, and make 
predictions. AI systems rely on large amounts of data during training and use. 
Data Set 
A collection of related data used for training, testing, or analyzing an AI system. 
Debugging 
The process of finding and fixing errors in computer code. AI can help students debug 
code, but students still need to understand the logic behind the fix.

===== PAGE 159 =====
Deep Learning 
A type of machine learning that uses layered systems called neural networks to recognize 
complex patterns in large amounts of data. 
Digital Literacy 
The ability to use digital tools effectively and responsibly. AI literacy is becoming an 
increasingly important part of digital literacy. 
Digital Tools 
Software or online platforms used to complete tasks such as writing, designing, 
organizing, studying, or communicating. 
Ethics 
Principles that guide responsible behavior and decision-making. In AI, ethics includes 
questions of fairness, honesty, privacy, and responsible use. 
Feedback Loop 
A cycle in which information about performance is used to improve the next attempt. 
AI can support learning by helping students work through feedback loops. 
Generative AI 
A type of AI that creates new content such as text, images, code, music, or video based 
on patterns it has learned from training data. 
Hallucination 
An AI-generated response that sounds believable but is inaccurate, misleading, or 
completely made up. Hallucinations are one reason students must verify important 
information. 
Human Judgment 
A person’s ability to evaluate information, consider context, weigh consequences, and 
make thoughtful decisions. Human judgment remains essential when using AI. 
Input 
The information given to a system. In AI, input may include prompts, uploaded text, 
questions, or instructions.

===== PAGE 160 =====
Innovation 
The process of creating or improving ideas, systems, or tools in ways that add value or 
solve problems. 
Iteration 
The process of improving something through repeated attempts. In AI use, students 
often improve results by refining prompts over time. 
Large Language Model (LLM) 
A type of AI trained on large amounts of text so it can recognize patterns in language 
and generate human-like responses. Many AI chat assistants are powered by large 
language models. 
Machine Learning 
A field of AI in which computers learn patterns from data rather than being explicitly 
programmed for every single task. 
Model 
A trained AI system that has learned patterns from data and can use those patterns to 
generate outputs such as predictions, text, or classifications. 
Natural Language Processing (NLP) 
A field of AI focused on helping computers understand, interpret, and generate human 
language. 
Neural Network 
A computing system inspired loosely by the structure of the human brain. Neural 
networks help AI systems detect patterns and make predictions. 
Output 
The response or result produced by a system after it processes input. 
Pattern Recognition 
The ability to identify repeated relationships or structures within data. This is a core part 
of how many AI systems work.

===== PAGE 161 =====
Plagiarism 
Presenting someone else’s words, ideas, or work as your own without proper credit. AI 
use can create plagiarism risks if students submit work they did not truly create or 
understand. 
Prediction 
The process by which an AI system estimates what word, idea, or result is most likely to 
come next based on learned patterns. 
Productivity 
The ability to complete tasks effectively and efficiently. AI can support productivity by 
helping students plan, organize, and manage work. 
Programming 
Writing instructions that tell a computer what to do. Programming is often done using 
coding languages such as Python or JavaScript. 
Prompt 
The question, instruction, or request given to an AI system. The quality of the prompt 
often affects the quality of the response. 
Prompt Engineering 
The skill of writing and improving prompts so that AI tools produce clearer, more 
accurate, or more useful results. 
Research 
The process of gathering, organizing, and evaluating information in order to answer a 
question or better understand a topic. 
Responsible AI Use 
Using AI in ways that are honest, thoughtful, and supportive of real learning. 
Responsible use includes verifying information, protecting privacy, and maintaining 
academic integrity.

===== PAGE 162 =====
Robotics 
A field of technology focused on machines that can perform physical tasks, often with 
some level of automation or AI support. 
Simulation 
A representation of a real-world process or scenario. AI can be used in simulations for 
training, learning, and experimentation. 
Source 
The place where information comes from, such as a book, website, teacher handout, 
article, or research database. 
Study Guide 
A structured review document that highlights the most important concepts, vocabulary, 
and ideas students should remember. 
Summarization 
The process of shortening information while keeping the main points. AI tools are often 
used to summarize notes, readings, or complex topics. 
Technology Literacy 
The ability to understand and use technology effectively. This includes not just operating 
tools, but knowing how and when to use them wisely. 
Training 
The process of teaching an AI model using large amounts of data so it can recognize 
patterns and perform useful tasks. 
Training Data 
The information used during the training of an AI system. The quality and variety of 
training data can affect how the system performs. 
Tutor 
A person—or in some cases, a tool—that helps guide learning, explain ideas, and 
support skill development. AI can act like a tutor when used well.

===== PAGE 163 =====
Verification 
The act of checking information to confirm that it is accurate and trustworthy. 
Workflow 
A sequence of steps used to complete a task. AI can help students understand or 
improve workflows in studying, writing, coding, and projects. 
Writing Prompt 
A question, instruction, or starting idea designed to help a person begin writing.
