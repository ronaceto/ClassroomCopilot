 copying AI interpretations without understanding or agreeing with them 
 submitting revised text that no longer sounds like the student’s own writing 
The question is not simply whether AI was used. The question is whether the student 
still did the reading, thinking, and writing that the assignment was meant to develop. 
Student Example 
A student named Ava is writing a literary analysis paragraph about symbolism in The 
Great Gatsby. She has an idea about what the green light represents, but she is struggling 
to organize the paragraph clearly. 
First, she asks AI: 
Help me brainstorm two possible interpretations of the green light in The Great 
Gatsby. 
After reviewing the ideas and comparing them with her notes, she writes her own 
paragraph. Then she asks: 
Suggest one way I can make this paragraph clearer without changing my 
interpretation. 
In this example, Ava still develops the claim and writes the analysis herself. AI helps her 
brainstorm and revise, but it does not replace her reading or thinking. 
Classroom Idea 
Teachers can help students use AI responsibly in ELA by having them compare three 
kinds of support: 
 a weak prompt that asks AI to do the writing 
 a stronger prompt that asks for brainstorming help 
 a revision prompt that keeps the student’s draft at the center 
Students can discuss which prompt supports learning most clearly and why. This helps 
them see that the quality and purpose of the prompt matter just as much as the tool 
itself.
